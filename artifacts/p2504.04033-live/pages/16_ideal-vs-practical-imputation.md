# Ideal vs. Practical Imputation Attacks

## TL;DR {#tldr}
An imputation attack tries to guess a hidden sensitive attribute using an auxiliary dataset the attacker already has. An "ideal" version of this attack assumes the attacker's auxiliary data matches the real training data's statistical properties almost perfectly — same correlations, same marginal priors, even within small subgroups. A "practical" version instead uses the kind of imperfect, mismatched auxiliary data a real-world attacker would realistically be able to obtain. The paper shows that this mismatch causes a large drop in attack accuracy, and that practical imputation attacks generally underperform model-based attacks like CSMIA and LOMIA — which is itself evidence that those model-based attacks are leaking real privacy information, not just exploiting a lucky auxiliary dataset.

## Intuition {#intuition}
Think of an ideal imputation attacker as someone who has a near-perfect twin of the private dataset — a second copy where every statistical relationship, down to small subgroups, looks the same as the original. A practical attacker is much more like a real adversary: they might scrape or purchase a dataset that resembles the target population only roughly, off in its overall proportions or in how features relate to each other within specific subgroups. The core question this concept addresses is: how much does that realism gap cost the attacker, and does it matter for judging whether a model is actually leaking privacy? The answer is that it matters a lot — because if even a somewhat-mismatched auxiliary dataset already loses badly to attacks that use no auxiliary data at all, that's a stronger signal that the model itself is the source of the leak, not the auxiliary data.

## Mechanics {#mechanics}
The experiments use the Adult dataset and probe two distinct kinds of distributional mismatch between an auxiliary dataset and the true training data. The first experiment introduces dataset-level (macro) drift by varying the auxiliary dataset's marginal prior — the fraction of positive samples — from 0.5 down to 0.1, moving it progressively further from the training data's true marginal prior of 0.52, while also varying auxiliary dataset size from 100 to 5000 records [§sec_6_2]. The second experiment instead holds the overall dataset-level correlation fixed at -0.44 (matching the training data's -0.4412) but introduces group-level (micro) drift, since the true training data's correlation varies widely across occupation groups, from -0.17 to -0.55 [§sec_6_2].

Performance is measured by comparing practical and ideal imputation attacks against the model-based CSMIA and LOMIA attacks. In the marginal-prior experiment, imputation attack accuracy declines sharply as the auxiliary prior deviates from 0.52, and at deviations of 0.1 and 0.2 the imputation attack falls below CSMIA (69.97) and LOMIA (70.61) regardless of how large the auxiliary dataset is — imputation only wins when its marginal prior stays close to the true one [§sec_6_2]. In the group-correlation experiment, restricted to occupation groups with higher-than-overall correlation, CSMIA and LOMIA outperform the practical imputation attack in three of the five most vulnerable groups, while the ideal imputation attack tracks CSMIA/LOMIA closely in two of those three groups [§sec_6_2].
