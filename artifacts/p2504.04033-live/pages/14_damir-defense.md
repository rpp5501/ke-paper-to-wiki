# Disparity-Aware Mutual Information Regularization (DAMIR)
## TL;DR {#tldr}
DAMIR is a defense strategy under Potential Mitigation Strategies that fixes a blind spot in standard mutual information regularization: instead of suppressing the link between a sensitive attribute and the model's output for everyone, it targets only the subgroup most vulnerable to attribute inference attacks. It contrasts with Balanced Correlation Defense (BCorr), another approach in the same family aimed at closing attack-success gaps between groups.

## Intuition {#intuition}
Mutual information regularization tries to make a model's output reveal less about a sensitive attribute by penalizing that statistical dependence during training. The problem is that applying this penalty uniformly across the whole population doesn't guarantee fairness in protection — one group can still end up far more exposed than another, and the blanket penalty can even make that gap worse while it hurts overall model accuracy. DAMIR's idea is simple: since disparity is really about one group being disproportionately vulnerable, only regularize the mutual information for that group's records, leaving the rest of the model free to retain accuracy.

## Mechanics {#mechanics}
The base method (MIR) adds a secondary loss term during training that minimizes mutual information between the sensitive attribute and the model's output, with a hyperparameter controlling how strongly this secondary objective is weighted against the primary training loss [§sec_7_1]. MIR does succeed at reducing untargeted attribute inference attack performance, but empirically it fails to reduce — and can even worsen — the disparity in attack success between groups [§sec_7_1]. DAMIR's key adjustment is to compute the mutual information loss only on records belonging to the vulnerable group, so the regularization pressure is concentrated where the disparity actually originates rather than spread across the whole dataset [§sec_7_1]. This was evaluated on a subset of the Census-19 dataset constructed so the Male and Female groups have correlations of -0.4 and -0.1 respectively with the sensitive attribute, sweeping the regularization hyperparameter from 0.001 to 0.4 and measuring both attack success rate difference (ASRD) between groups and target model accuracy [§sec_7_1]. Under the CSMIA attack, DAMIR modestly outperforms MIR, reducing disparity to some degree without the same drop in utility, but still requires substantial accuracy loss to fully eliminate disparity [§sec_7_1]. Under the LOMIA attack, results are worse still — the evaluation finds no way to meaningfully reduce disparity without a significant utility hit, leading to the conclusion that mutual information regularization (even in its disparity-aware form) is not an effective disparity mitigation [§sec_7_1].
