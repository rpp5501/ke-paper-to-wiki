# SID versus SHD Simulation
## TL;DR {#tldr}
This concept reports a simulation study comparing Structural Intervention Distance (SID) against Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) on random pairs of DAGs, showing that the two metrics capture fundamentally different notions of graph dissimilarity. Because SID is part of the broader Structural Intervention Distance (SID) framework, this study serves as its empirical validation, and it builds on prior work comparing causal inference methods by demonstrating why a purely edge-counting metric like SHD is insufficient for judging causal accuracy. The headline finding is that graphs with very low SHD (differing by only one or two edges) can still have wildly different SID values, meaning SHD is a poor proxy for how well a graph estimate would guide causal decisions.

## Intuition {#intuition}
Imagine two estimated causal graphs that differ from the true graph by just a single misplaced edge — SHD would call them "almost identical." But if that one edge sits in a critical position, it can completely flip the predicted effect of intervening on one variable to change another, so downstream causal conclusions could be totally wrong. SID versus SHD Simulation is the experiment designed to expose exactly this gap: it generates many random graph pairs, measures both distances, and asks whether low SHD reliably predicts low SID. It doesn't — SHD and SID diverge, confirming that counting edge differences is not the same as counting wrong causal conclusions, which is the more relevant question for evaluating causal graph estimates.

## Mechanics {#mechanics}
The simulation procedure samples pairs of random DAGs under two edge-density regimes — a sparse setting and a dense setting — with edges drawn i.i.d. according to a chosen probability, and vertex orderings drawn uniformly at random from all permutations [§sec_3_1]. For each sampled pair, both SID and SHD are computed, and the results are visualized as two-dimensional histograms comparing the two metrics side by side for the sparse and dense regimes [§sec_3_1]. The key empirical observation is that for a fixed low SHD value (e.g., one or two, most visible in the dense setting), the corresponding SID values are spread across a wide range, showing that SHD and SID behave as genuinely different distance measures and that SID carries additional information relevant to causal inference [§sec_3_1]. To connect this to actual causal correctness, each graph pair is paired with a linear structural equation model with Gaussian, equal-variance noise, which makes the DAG identifiable from the observational distribution and lets the true causal effect between any two variables be summarized by a single number (the derivative of the conditional expectation, i.e., the total causal effect) [§sec_3_1]. Comparing this true causal effect number between the two graphs in a pair, and counting how often they differ beyond a numerical tolerance, produces a ground-truth count of "wrongly estimated causal effects," and the simulation shows that SID matches this count exactly in all examined cases [§sec_3_1]. The authors note this exact match is not guaranteed by the SID definition itself, since the definition only requires the existence of some discriminating distribution, and they identify two mechanisms — a effect difference smaller than the numerical threshold, and vanishing coefficients that would violate faithfulness — as the rare cases where equality could fail without being detected [§sec_3_1].
