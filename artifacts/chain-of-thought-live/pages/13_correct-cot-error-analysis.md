# Correct Chain of Thought Analysis
## TL;DR {#tldr}

The paper manually audits 50 chain-of-thought explanations from the 137B model that reached a correct final answer on GSM8K [§sec_14_1]. Only one of the 50 was right by chance; the other 49 had correct reasoning, and 44 of those were entirely flawless [§sec_14_1]. The authors argue this near-perfect correctness rate holds mainly for free-response math, not for multiple-choice or binary tasks [§sec_14_1].

## Intuition {#intuition}

A correct final answer does not guarantee correct reasoning: a model can stumble into the right number while its explanation is nonsense [§sec_14_1].

**Free-response math limits lucky guesses:** GSM8K answers are arbitrary numbers, so a chain of thought that reasons incorrectly is unlikely to land on the exact right value by coincidence [§sec_14_1].

**Multiple-choice and binary tasks remove that safeguard:** with only a handful of options, an incorrect reasoning path can still land on the correct label, so a correct answer says much less about the reasoning that produced it [§sec_14_1].

## Mechanics {#mechanics}

The authors sampled 50 chains of thought generated by the 137B model that reached a correct final answer on GSM8K, then manually judged each chain's logic and arithmetic [§sec_14_1].

| Outcome | Count | Share of 50 |
|---|---|---|
| Correct logic and math, no imperfections | 44 | 88% [§sec_14_1] |
| Correct logic and math, minor imperfections | 5 | 10% [§sec_14_1] |
| Correct by chance (incorrect reasoning) | 1 | 2% [§sec_14_1] |

Only one of the 50 chains reached the correct answer through incorrect reasoning — a case the authors label correct by chance [§sec_14_1].

The remaining 49 chains had correct logic and correct math, though five carried a minor imperfection without breaking the reasoning's coherence [§sec_14_1].

| Imperfection type | Count |
|---|---|
| Underspecified statements | 1 [§sec_14_1] |
| Unrelated but correct statement | 1 [§sec_14_1] |
| Omitted equation steps, still understandable | 2 [§sec_14_1] |
| Correct math with inverted semantics | 1 [§sec_14_1] |

The authors read this near-total correctness as a property of free-response math questions specifically: GSM8K's numeric answers give a wrong reasoning path very little chance of landing on the exact right number [§sec_14_1].

They contrast this with multiple-choice and binary tasks, including every commonsense reasoning dataset they evaluate, where an incorrect reasoning path is far more likely to still produce the correct label [§sec_14_1].

The authors call this a limitation of their analysis rather than a settled result [§sec_14_1].

They recommend future work that directly checks the factuality of chain-of-thought explanations, rather than inferring correctness only from whether the final answer matches [§sec_14_1].

## The Math {#the-math}

Take the 50 audited chains as the whole sample: 49 (98%) got the logic and math right, split into 44 (88%) with no flaws and 5 (10%) with a minor slip, while 1 (2%) reached the right number by faulty reasoning [§sec_14_1].

Those three groups sum back to 50, so the audit accounts for every sampled chain [§sec_14_1].

This 2% chance-correct rate is a ceiling only for a task whose answer space is effectively unbounded: GSM8K's numeric answers make hitting the exact right value from wrong reasoning rare [§sec_14_1].

A multiple-choice or binary task shrinks that answer space to a handful of options, so the same faulty-reasoning process can land on the correct label far more often — which is exactly the contrast the paper draws with its commonsense reasoning datasets [§sec_14_1].

The 5 minor-imperfection chains break down as 1 underspecified statement + 1 unrelated-but-correct statement + 2 with omitted equation steps + 1 with inverted semantics, which sums to 5 and matches the count above [§sec_14_1].

## Go Deeper {#go-deeper}

This audit is a qualitative complement to the quantitative arithmetic-reasoning accuracy numbers reported for the same model: it shows the headline accuracy gain is backed by mostly sound reasoning, not by the model getting lucky on free-response numeric answers [§sec_14_1].

The authors flag their own sample as too small and too narrow to settle the factuality question, and they call for future work that audits chain-of-thought factuality directly instead of inferring it from final-answer correctness [§sec_14_1].

That gap matters most for multiple-choice and binary-classification tasks, where the audit's own logic — that free-response answers are hard to guess into — does not hold, so a correct answer there is a much weaker signal of correct reasoning [§sec_14_1].
