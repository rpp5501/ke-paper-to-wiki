# From Symmetry to Conserved Quantities
## TL;DR {#tldr}
A continuous symmetry of the loss — a direction you can move a parameter without changing the loss value — hands gradient flow a quantity it cannot change. In deep linear networks that quantity is *imbalance*, the difference between adjacent layers' Gram matrices, and analogous quantities exist for homogeneous activations, attention layers, convolutions, and ResNet blocks [§sec_5_1].

## Intuition {#intuition}
Think of the loss surface as a landscape with a valley that runs perfectly flat in some direction — that flat direction is the symmetry. Gradient flow always steps perpendicular to level sets of the loss, so it can never drift along a flat direction on its own; whatever coordinate measures "where you are along that flat direction" is frozen for the whole trajectory. That frozen coordinate is the conserved quantity, the neural-network analogue of Noether's theorem pairing continuous symmetries with conservation laws in physics [§sec_5_1].

## Mechanics {#mechanics}
**Gradient flow and conservation, defined.** Gradient flow is the continuous-time limit of gradient descent: a trajectory $\theta(t)$ that moves along the negative loss gradient and connects an initialization to a critical point. A conserved quantity is any function $Q$ of the parameters that has the same value at every point on that trajectory [§sec_5_1].

**The flagship example is imbalance.** For an $l$-layer linear feedforward network, the difference between the Gram matrix of one layer's weights and the next layer's is invariant under gradient flow — it never changes value as training proceeds, no matter how the individual layer weights move [§sec_5_1].

**The same pattern recurs across architectures**, each time as a difference between "incoming" and "outgoing" quantities at a layer or neuron:

| Setting | Conserved quantity |
|---|---|
| Linear feedforward networks | Imbalance: Gram-matrix difference between adjacent layers [§sec_5_1] |
| Feedforward nets, homogeneous activations | Squared-norm difference between each neuron's incoming and outgoing weights [§sec_5_1] |
| Graph attention networks, homogeneous activations | Difference between incoming and outgoing parameter norms per neuron [§sec_5_1] |
| Convolutional layers / ResNet blocks | Analogous differences, derived by rewriting conv layers as equivalent fully-connected layers [§sec_5_1] |
| Natural gradient descent, curvature-corrected dynamics | Differences and ratios of weight-matrix singular values under spectral initialization [§sec_5_1] |
| Radial rescaling activation layers | An angular-momentum-like quantity, new relative to the homogeneous-activation cases [§sec_5_1] |

**A body of work makes the Noether connection explicit rather than case-by-case.** One line of work treats one-parameter symmetry groups — translation, scaling, rescaling — as the generators and derives their conserved quantities directly, paralleling Noether's theorem in both form and intuition; a follow-up extends this to higher-dimensional symmetry groups by writing optimizers as ODEs, identifying a Lagrangian, and applying Noether's theorem through the symmetry group's generators, which recovers imbalance and extends conservation laws to first- and second-order dynamics including Newtonian dynamics and Nesterov's accelerated flows [§sec_5_1]. A third approach associates conserved quantities with infinitesimal symmetries of general equivariant activation functions, recovering the homogeneous-activation results as a special case [§sec_5_1].

**Whether the known conserved quantities are the only ones is still open.** The Frobenius theorem lets you count the independent conserved quantities generated by the Lie algebra of vector fields spanned by the model's Jacobian; applied to matrix factorization (linear networks without bias), this count shows imbalance is the *only* independent conservation law, and the same holds for attention layers in transformers [§sec_5_1].

## The Math {#the-math}
Gradient flow's velocity is the negative loss gradient:

$$\dot{\theta}(t) = - \nabla_{\theta(t)} L$$
[§sec_5_1]

A conserved quantity holds this value fixed along the whole trajectory:

$$Q(\theta(s))=Q(\theta(t)) \quad \text{for all } s,t \in \R_{\geq 0}$$
[§sec_5_1]

**Why orthogonality is exactly the condition that makes this work:**

```derivation
shape: A symmetry direction tangent to the loss level set forces its matching Q to be conserved under gradient flow.
steps:
  - latex: "\\dot Q(\\theta(t)) = \\nabla Q(\\theta(t)) \\cdot \\dot\\theta(t)"
    why: "Chain rule applied to Q along the trajectory [§sec_5_1]"
  - latex: "\\dot\\theta(t) = -\\nabla_{\\theta(t)} L"
    why: "Gradient flow's defining equation [§sec_5_1]"
  - latex: "\\dot Q(\\theta(t)) = -\\nabla Q(\\theta(t)) \\cdot \\nabla L(\\theta(t))"
    why: "Substitute the gradient flow velocity into the chain rule [§sec_5_1]"
  - latex: "\\nabla Q(\\theta(t)) \\perp \\nabla L(\\theta(t)) \\;\\Rightarrow\\; \\dot Q(\\theta(t)) = 0"
    why: "A symmetry direction lies tangent to the loss level set and is therefore orthogonal to ∇L; if ∇Q is matched to that direction, the dot product vanishes exactly, and Q's time-derivative is zero for the entire flow, not just approximately [S1][S2]"
```

For linear feedforward networks, each layer-pair $h \in [l-1]$ has an imbalance matrix:

$$W^{(h)} (W^{(h)})^T - (W^{(h+1)})^T W^{(h+1)} \in \R^{n_h \times n_h}$$
[§sec_5_1]

**A minimal case makes this concrete.** Take a 2-layer scalar network $f = w_2 w_1 x$, so $l=2$ and there is one imbalance value, $w_1^2 - w_2^2$. The rescaling symmetry $w_1 \to c\,w_1,\; w_2 \to w_2/c$ leaves the product $w_2 w_1$ — and hence the loss — unchanged for any $c \neq 0$, which is exactly the flat direction the theorem is about. Starting from $w_1=2, w_2=3$ gives imbalance $4-9=-5$; gradient flow may move $w_1$ and $w_2$ anywhere along their joint trajectory, but $w_1(t)^2 - w_2(t)^2 = -5$ holds at every $t$, constraining which critical point the flow can reach [§sec_5_1].

**A boundary worth noting: this is a continuous-time statement.** The derivation above is exact for gradient flow, but discrete gradient descent takes finite steps rather than following $\nabla L$ infinitesimally, so the conservation law is broken by an amount controlled by the step size — it holds only approximately for small learning rates, and that approximation error is itself something that can be bounded and analyzed [S1][S2].
