# Distribution Symmetry
## TL;DR {#tldr}

Distribution symmetry only requires a parameter transformation to preserve the *expected* value of a function $F$ over a data distribution $D$ — not its value at every individual input [eq_6]. It sits at the bottom of the paper's implication hierarchy: the weakest, most permissive symmetry notion it defines [§sec_2_3_3].

## Intuition {#intuition}

A class average can stay the same even though individual grades shift, as long as the shifts cancel out. Distribution symmetry works the same way: a transformation counts as symmetric if it leaves the *average* behavior of the network unchanged over the data distribution, even when it changes the output at individual points [§sec_2_3_3].

This averaging is exactly why the definition sits below neural network symmetry and loss symmetry in the paper's hierarchy: those require agreement at every single input, while distribution symmetry only requires agreement on average [§sec_2_3_3].

Distribution symmetry builds on data-dependent symmetry, which makes the same averaging argument over one fixed, finite batch instead of the full population [§sec_2_3_3]. As the batch grows to cover the whole distribution, a data-dependent symmetry increasingly reflects a genuine distribution symmetry [S2].

## Mechanics {#mechanics}

Fix a function $F \colon \Par \times \Data \to Y$ and a data distribution $D$. A distribution symmetry is a group $G$ acting on the parameter space $\Par$ such that transforming the parameter by any $g \in G$ leaves $\E_{x \sim D}[F(\cdot, x)]$ unchanged [eq_6].

The paper illustrates this with linear regression: $F(W,(x,y)) = Wx - y$, where $W \in \R^{m \times n}$ is the parameter, $x$ is drawn from $D_x$, and the label is generated by a fixed true matrix, $y = W^*x$ [§sec_2_3_3].

Taking the expectation over $D_x$ collapses every sample $x$ down to its mean $\bar{x} = \E_{x\sim D_x}[x]$, so $\E_{x\sim D_x}[F(W,(x,y))]$ reduces to the single expression $W\bar{x} - W^*\bar{x}$ [eq_7].

Let $G$ be the group of invertible matrices $A$ satisfying $A\bar{x} = \bar{x}$, acting on parameters by $A \cdot W = WA^{-1}$. Any such $A$ is a distribution symmetry, because substituting $A \cdot W$ for $W$ leaves that reduced expression unchanged [eq_8].

This invariance holds only in expectation. Pick a single sample $x_0 \ne \bar{x}$: then $F(A\cdot W,(x_0,y_0)) = WA^{-1}x_0 - W^*x_0$ generally differs from $F(W,(x_0,y_0)) = Wx_0 - W^*x_0$, since $A^{-1}$ need not fix $x_0$ itself [eq_8].

So the same transformation that is exactly symmetric for the distribution can change predictions on any single data point — this is the boundary case that separates distribution symmetry from the pointwise definitions above it [§sec_2_3_3].

The paper's implication hierarchy places distribution symmetry as the weakest condition: neural network symmetry implies loss symmetry, and both imply their data-dependent and distribution-level counterparts, with no reverse implications [§sec_2_3_3].

## The Math {#the-math}

The general definition requires the expectation of $F$ to be invariant under the group action, for every parameter and every group element [eq_6]:

$$\E_{x \sim D} [F(g \cdot , x)] = \E_{x \sim D} [F(, x)],\quad \forall g \in G,\quad \forall  \in \Par.$$
[eq_6]

The linear-regression example turns this abstract invariance into a concrete algebraic identity. Expanding $\E_{x\sim D_x}[F(W,(x,y))]$ first isolates the quantity that must stay fixed [eq_7]:

$$\E_{x \sim D_x} [F(W, (x, y))] 
        = \E_{x \sim D_x} [Wx - W^* x] 
        = W \bar{x} - W^* \bar{x}.$$
[eq_7]

## Go Deeper {#go-deeper}

- [Exchangeable random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchangeable_random_variables) — start here: exchangeability is the simplest example of a distribution-level symmetry, and de Finetti's theorem shows what structure it forces.
- [Probabilistic Symmetries and Invariant Neural Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06082) (Bloem-Reddy & Teh) — the formal treatment of symmetry as a group action on a distribution, including the invariant/equivariant representation theorems behind this definition.
- [Group Equivariant Convolutional Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07576) (Cohen & Welling) — shows concretely how building a distribution symmetry into a model's architecture shrinks the hypothesis space and can improve generalization.
